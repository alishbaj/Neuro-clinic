“It’s just a bad headache” is the phrase I hear most often from people who turn out to have migraine — frequently after years of treating it with over-the-counter painkillers and a dark room.

The distinction is not academic. Migraine and tension-type headache respond to different treatments, carry different risks, and require completely different long-term plans. Getting the label right is most of the work.

The features that separate them

No single feature is decisive on its own, but the pattern usually is. Most people can place themselves fairly accurately once they know what to look for.

Migraine typically involves:

  • Moderate to severe pain, often — though not always — on one side of the head
  • A throbbing or pulsating quality
  • Worsening with routine physical activity, so that climbing stairs or bending over makes it noticeably worse
  • Nausea, with or without vomiting
  • Marked sensitivity to light, sound, or both
  • Attacks lasting anywhere from four hours to three days if untreated
  • A strong urge to lie down in the dark and stop doing whatever you were doing

Tension-type headache typically involves:

  • Mild to moderate pain, usually on both sides
  • A pressing or tightening quality — a band around the head rather than a pulse
  • No worsening with ordinary activity; most people carry on working through it
  • No nausea, and at most one of light or sound sensitivity

The most useful discriminator in ordinary clinic practice is disability. If a headache regularly stops you doing what you were doing, it is very unlikely to be a simple tension headache, whatever it is called at home.

Aura, and what it is not

Roughly a quarter to a third of people with migraine experience aura: reversible neurological symptoms that usually precede the headache and typically build over several minutes and resolve within an hour.

Visual aura is the most common form — a shimmering or zigzag arc, an expanding blind spot, flickering lights that spread gradually across the visual field. Sensory aura produces tingling that marches slowly up an arm or across the face. Speech aura causes temporary difficulty finding words.

That gradual spread is the key. Aura creeps over minutes. Stroke symptoms arrive all at once, fully formed. If neurological symptoms come on abruptly, or if an aura-like episode is happening for the first time in your life, that is a reason to call 911 rather than to wait it out. Migraine with aura also carries a modestly increased stroke risk, which makes it worth discussing with a physician — particularly around contraceptive choices and smoking.

Red flags that change the conversation

Most headaches are primary headaches, meaning the headache is the condition rather than a symptom of something else. But some patterns need imaging and urgent assessment rather than reassurance.

Seek prompt evaluation for:

  • A thunderclap headache — maximum intensity within a minute of onset. This is an emergency.
  • The worst headache of your life, or a headache clearly different in character from your usual ones.
  • A new headache after the age of 50.
  • Headache with fever, neck stiffness, confusion, seizure, or weakness or numbness that does not resolve.
  • Headache that is consistently worse when lying flat, or that wakes you from sleep.
  • Headache that is steadily escalating in frequency or severity over weeks.
  • New headache in someone with cancer, HIV, or immunosuppression, or after a head injury.

Medication overuse: the trap almost everyone falls into

This is the single most common reversible reason someone’s headaches have become daily, and it is the most counter-intuitive thing I explain in clinic.

Taking acute headache medication too often causes headaches. As a rough guide, simple analgesics on more than 15 days a month, or triptans, combination analgesics or opioids on more than 10 days a month, can tip a manageable episodic pattern into a chronic daily one. The medication still relieves each individual headache, which is precisely why the cycle is so hard to see from the inside.

The way out is to withdraw the overused medication while starting an appropriate preventive — and it is worth doing with a physician, because things often get worse for a stretch before they improve.

How treatment actually differs

For tension-type headache, treatment centres on simple analgesics used sparingly, addressing posture, sleep, stress and jaw clenching, and a preventive such as amitriptyline where headaches are frequent.

For migraine, treatment has two arms that work together. Acute treatment aborts an attack in progress — triptans, gepants, ditans, anti-nausea medication, taken early rather than after hours of waiting. Preventive treatment reduces how often attacks happen at all, and is worth considering once attacks occur on roughly four or more days a month or are severely disabling. Options include beta blockers, topiramate, candesartan, amitriptyline, CGRP monoclonal antibodies and, for chronic migraine specifically, botulinum toxin (BOTOX®) injection.

That last option applies to patients with headaches on 15 or more days a month, delivered on a fixed protocol every twelve weeks. It is one of the better-evidenced treatments in chronic migraine, and it is offered here.

Keep a headache diary

Bring one to your appointment and you will get considerably more out of it. Record the date, how long the headache lasted, the severity out of ten, what you took and when, whether it worked, and anything notable about sleep, stress, meals or menstrual cycle in the preceding day.

Two things become visible in a diary that are nearly invisible from memory: how many days a month you are actually taking acute medication, and whether a preventive is genuinely working. People consistently underestimate the first and are consistently unsure about the second.

When to see a neurologist

Come in if headaches are interfering with work, family or sleep; if you are using acute medication on more than about ten days a month; if over-the-counter treatment has stopped working; if your headache pattern has changed; or if you have any of the red flags above.

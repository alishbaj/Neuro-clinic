Stroke is the fifth leading cause of death in the United States and a leading cause of long-term adult disability. It is also one of the few neurological emergencies where what happens in the first hour genuinely changes the outcome.

The reason is arithmetic. In a large-vessel ischemic stroke, an estimated 1.9 million neurons die every minute that blood flow is not restored. Every hour of delay ages the affected brain by roughly 3.6 years. Treatments that dissolve or remove the clot work extremely well — but only inside a narrow window, and only for people who get to a stroke-capable hospital in time.

Which means the single most important link in the chain is not a machine or a medication. It is somebody in the room recognising what they are looking at and calling 911.

BE FAST: the six warning signs

The older FAST mnemonic captured face, arm, speech and time. BE FAST adds two signs that FAST misses — and those two account for a meaningful share of strokes in the back of the brain, which are the ones most often sent home from emergency departments undiagnosed.

Call 911 immediately if any one of these appears suddenly:

  • B — Balance. Sudden loss of balance or coordination, or a sudden inability to walk straight.
  • E — Eyes. Sudden loss of vision in one or both eyes, or sudden double vision.
  • F — Face. Sudden drooping or numbness on one side of the face. Ask the person to smile and look for asymmetry.
  • A — Arms. Sudden weakness or numbness in one arm. Ask the person to raise both arms and see whether one drifts down.
  • S — Speech. Sudden slurred speech, trouble finding words, or trouble understanding what is being said. Ask them to repeat a simple sentence.
  • T — Time. If any of these are present, note the time symptoms started and call 911.

The word that matters most in that list is sudden. Numbness that has crept up over three months is a different problem. Numbness that appeared over three minutes is a stroke until proven otherwise.

Why the start time matters so much

Treatment eligibility is measured from the moment the person was last known to be well — not from the moment someone noticed. That distinction decides who is eligible for clot-dissolving medication and who is not.

If someone wakes with symptoms, the clock starts when they went to sleep, not when they woke up. If they were fine at dinner and slurring at nine, the clock starts at dinner. Write the time down. It is the first thing the stroke team will ask, and in the noise of an emergency it is remarkably easy to lose.

Call 911. Do not drive.

This is the part patients push back on most, and it is the part I am most insistent about.

Driving to the hospital costs you time you cannot get back:

  • Paramedics begin the assessment in the ambulance and alert the hospital before you arrive, so the stroke team is assembled and the scanner is clear when you get there.
  • They route you to a hospital that can actually treat stroke, which is not always the nearest one.
  • If the person deteriorates on the way — vomiting, seizing, losing consciousness — a car is the worst possible place for that to happen.

Patients who arrive by ambulance are treated significantly faster than those who arrive by private car. Do not drive yourself under any circumstances: a stroke can affect vision, judgement and the use of a limb, all of which you are relying on at that moment.

TIA: the warning you should not be relieved by

A transient ischemic attack produces the same symptoms but they resolve, often within minutes. The relief people feel afterwards is completely understandable and slightly dangerous, because a TIA carries a substantial short-term risk of a full stroke — with much of that risk concentrated in the first 48 hours.

A TIA is not a false alarm. It is the one warning you get, and it deserves urgent evaluation rather than a wait-and-see appointment next month. The workup looks for the cause — a narrowed carotid artery, atrial fibrillation, a cardiac source, small-vessel disease — because the cause determines the prevention.

Preventing the next one

Once the acute event is over, stroke care becomes a long game played largely between appointments. The modifiable risk factors are well established and, taken together, account for the large majority of stroke risk.

What actually moves the needle:

  • Blood pressure. The single largest modifiable risk factor for stroke. Treating hypertension substantially reduces risk, and home monitoring beats occasional office readings.
  • Atrial fibrillation. An irregular heart rhythm dramatically raises stroke risk, and appropriate anticoagulation removes most of that excess risk. AF is often silent, which is why it is worth looking for.
  • Cholesterol. Lowering LDL reduces recurrent ischemic stroke, particularly where atherosclerosis is the mechanism.
  • Diabetes and blood sugar control. Both raise risk and worsen outcomes after a stroke.
  • Smoking. Stopping produces a measurable fall in risk, and the benefit begins within the first year.
  • Sleep apnoea. Frequently overlooked, strongly associated with stroke, and treatable.
  • Physical activity and weight. Regular activity lowers risk directly and improves nearly every factor above.

None of these are dramatic interventions. They are unglamorous, cumulative and genuinely effective — which is exactly why the follow-up visit matters as much as the emergency one.

When to see a neurologist

Come in if you have had a stroke or TIA and want the cause properly worked up; if you have several vascular risk factors and want to know where you actually stand; or if you have had brief episodes of weakness, numbness, vision loss or speech difficulty that you have been quietly explaining away.
